Understanding Food Labels

Food labels provide valuable information about the nutritional content of packaged foods. Learning how to read and interpret these labels can help you make healthier food choices.

Anatomy of a Nutrition Facts Label

The Nutrition Facts label is required on most packaged foods. Here's what each section means:

1. Serving Information

This section shows how many servings are in the package and the serving size. All the nutrition information on the label is based on one serving, so if you eat more or less than the serving size, you'll need to adjust the values accordingly.

Important Tip

Always check the serving size first! Many packages contain multiple servings, and the nutritional information is based on a single serving, not the entire package.

2. Calories

This shows the total number of calories in one serving of the food. Calories provide a measure of how much energy you get from a serving of the food.

3. Nutrients to Limit

These are nutrients that Americans generally consume in adequate or excessive amounts, and overconsumption may increase the risk of certain chronic diseases:

  • Total Fat, Saturated Fat, and Trans Fat: High intake of saturated and trans fats is linked to increased risk of heart disease.
  • Cholesterol: Too much cholesterol can increase the risk of heart disease.
  • Sodium: High sodium intake can lead to high blood pressure.
  • Added Sugars: These contribute calories but few or no nutrients.

4. Nutrients to Get Enough Of

These are nutrients that many Americans don't get enough of, and inadequate intake may be associated with health concerns:

  • Dietary Fiber: Important for digestive health and may help reduce the risk of heart disease.
  • Vitamin D: Essential for bone health and immune function.
  • Calcium: Important for bone health.
  • Iron: Essential for carrying oxygen in the blood.
  • Potassium: Helps maintain healthy blood pressure.

5. % Daily Value (%DV)

The %DV shows how much a nutrient in a serving of food contributes to a total daily diet. The %DV helps you determine if a serving of food is high or low in a nutrient.

Quick Guide to %DV

  • 5% DV or less: Low in that nutrient
  • 20% DV or more: High in that nutrient

Ingredient List

The ingredient list shows all ingredients in a food product in descending order by weight. This means the first ingredient listed is present in the largest amount.

Reading Ingredient Lists

  • Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight
  • Look for products with shorter ingredient lists
  • Be cautious of products with many unfamiliar ingredients
  • Watch out for different forms of sugar (ingredients ending in "-ose" like fructose, dextrose, etc.)

Common Food Label Claims and What They Mean

Claim What It Means
Low Calorie 40 calories or less per serving
Low Fat 3g or less of fat per serving
Low Sodium 140mg or less of sodium per serving
High Fiber 5g or more of fiber per serving
Good Source of The food provides 10-19% of the Daily Value for a particular nutrient
Excellent Source of The food provides 20% or more of the Daily Value for a particular nutrient
Light/Lite The product contains 1/3 fewer calories or 50% less fat than the regular product
Natural The product does not contain artificial ingredients, preservatives, or color additives
Organic The product meets specific USDA organic standards for production, handling, and processing

Using Food Labels Effectively

  • Compare similar products to find the healthier option
  • Look beyond the front of the package claims and check the Nutrition Facts label
  • Consider your personal health needs when evaluating products
  • Remember that whole, unprocessed foods often don't have nutrition labels but are generally healthy choices
